The numbers, with their caveats.

Two kinds of numbers live here. My own 30 days of Claude Code, aggregated: 408 sessions, 71,511 requests, no prompts and no project names. And the parts agento is built from: how far an LLM judge can be trusted to pick a model, and how the first trained classifier did.

None of it is a measured saving, and none of it says anything about your month. npx agento-cc audit prints your own version of the first half. A public benchmark, cost per resolved task against always-Opus, opusplan and rule routers, is not done yet.

Where my month went

$8,175 at API list prices over 30 days, two thirds of it cache reads: the long context read again on every step.1 The cache hit ratio was 99.2%, so this is not a cache that fails. It is a cache that works and is read very often.

1API-equivalent. I am on a subscription, which charges nothing per token; the real constraint is the weekly limit. Prices as of 2026-10-05.

Week by week, and who spent it

On a subscription the heaviest week is what runs into the limit, not the monthly total.

Subagents were 12% of the spend. Their model is the one choice that is always free to make, because each subagent starts with its own cache.2

2“Explore” is Claude Code’s built-in read-only subagent. The audit suggests Haiku for it.

Where the cache was lost

Rewriting the cache cost an estimated $301 over the month, and $222 of that came from 83 pauses longer than the cache lives. A model switch caused only four rewrites, because I rarely switched.3

3These are the audit’s estimates. “Cause not visible” means the transcript did not show why the cache was written again.

What a mid-task switch would have cost

The audit replays my own steps as if the model had been switched in the middle: the penalty is the whole cached prefix written again for the new model, and the gain is the cheaper price of each later step.

The replay assumes the cheaper model finishes in as many steps as the original, which it often does not.4 That is why the model is picked at a clean point, where there is no cache to lose, and why work inside a task goes to subagents.

4The worked example with list prices is on How it works.

What ran, and what a judge says was needed

Claude Opus read 576 of my finished tasks (the prompt, the diff and a compressed trajectory) and named the cheapest model it thinks would have done as well. Of the 560 that ran on Opus or Fable, it said 303, or 54%, would have been fine on Sonnet or Haiku.5

5A judge’s opinion of a finished task, not a replay. Nobody re-ran these tasks on a cheaper model.

How far a judge can be trusted

A router can be wrong in two directions. Picking a model that is more expensive than needed only loses the saving: you pay what you paid before. Picking one that is too cheap costs quality: a broken change, a retry, a debugging loop that eats more than the saving. So the number that matters is how often a judge picks too cheap.

6The hand labels are mine, on my own past tasks: 34 for Opus, 19 for Qwen. The SWE-bench row uses step prefixes from TwinRouterBench, whose reference labels are weak.

Against my own labels, the Opus judge picks too cheap 41% of the time. That is why judge labels are checked against hand labels and replays before anything is trained on them, and why none of these numbers is a promise.

The first training run

Run opus-v1 distilled the Opus judge’s labels into a student small enough to answer on a laptop: multilingual-e5-small, taught by a larger multilingual teacher.7 The student answers four questions at a clean point. Two of the answers are good enough and are in use; the model choice is not.

7Teacher: Laya multilingual (mmBERT, 322M), 6 epochs, 236 s on one RTX PRO 4000. 576 tasks split by time: 407 train, 87 calibration, 87 test.

To pick the model on its own, the student has to stay under 5% too-cheap picks on held-out tasks at some confidence threshold. No threshold got there.8

8The labels are the Opus judge’s opinions, the same judge that picks too cheap 41% of the time against my labels. The student learned the judge, not the truth.

L0 is the interesting row. It is a simple heuristic that looks at the finished trajectory, so it cannot run at the start of a task, but it agrees with the labels 75% of the time with 4.6% too-cheap picks. The first steps of a task say more than the prompt. That is the next experiment: decide after the first few steps, when those signals exist.
